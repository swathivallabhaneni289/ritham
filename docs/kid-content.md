## 1. Food Ideas

**Source:** United States Department of Agriculture (USDA), Center for Nutrition Policy and
Promotion — *"10 tips Nutrition Education Series: veggies and fruits — 10 tips for making healthy
foods more fun for children."* DG TipSheet No. 11, June 2011. Publicly published at
ChooseMyPlate.gov (USDA's own MyPlate nutrition education series). Retrieved via a third-party
extension-office mirror of the PDF during this research pass, not usda.gov directly — the
publisher, tipsheet number, and date are confirmed from the document's own printed USDA/CNPP block
at the bottom of the PDF page (not from the mirror site's own framing), but re-pull the source
directly from usda.gov/ChooseMyPlate.gov before Phase 5 sign-off to confirm no revision has since
superseded this text.

Preamble sentence, transcribed verbatim: *"Encourage children to eat vegetables and fruits by
making it fun. Provide healthy ingredients and let kids help with preparation, based on their age
and skills. Kids may try foods they avoided in the past if they helped make them."*

Ten ideas, transcribed verbatim (tip titles are the source document's own bolded headings; body
text is the source's own tip text, reproduced word for word). **Numbering and presentation order
below are Ritham's own, for readability — the source PDF's own tip numbering is a decorative
layout device (tips are scattered across the page, not printed in a linear ranked list), not a
meaningful order to preserve:**

1. **Smoothie creations** — "Blend fat-free or low-fat yogurt or milk with fruit pieces and
   crushed ice. Use fresh, frozen, canned, and even overripe fruits. Try bananas, berries,
   peaches, and/or pineapple. If you freeze the fruit first, you can even skip the ice."
2. **Delicious dippers** — "Kids love to dip their foods. Whip up a quick dip for veggies with
   yogurt and seasonings such as herbs or garlic. Serve with raw vegetables like broccoli,
   carrots, or cauliflower. Fruit chunks go great with a yogurt and cinnamon or vanilla dip."
3. **Caterpillar kabobs** — "Assemble chunks of melon, apple, orange, and pear on skewers for a
   fruity kabob. For a raw veggie version, use vegetables like zucchini, cucumber, squash, sweet
   peppers, or tomatoes."
4. **Personalized pizzas** — "Set up a pizza-making station in the kitchen. Use whole-wheat
   English muffins, bagels, or pita bread as the crust. Have tomato sauce, low-fat cheese, and
   cut-up vegetables or fruits for toppings. Let kids choose their own favorites. Then pop the
   pizzas into the oven to warm."
5. **Fruity peanut butterfly** — "Start with carrot sticks or celery for the body. Attach wings
   made of thinly sliced apples with peanut butter and decorate with halved grapes or dried
   fruit."
6. **Frosty fruits** — "Frozen treats are bound to be popular in the warm months. Just put fresh
   fruits such as melon chunks in the freezer (rinse first). Make 'popsicles' by inserting sticks
   into peeled bananas and freezing."
7. **Bugs on a log** — "Use celery, cucumber, or carrot sticks as the log and add peanut butter.
   Top with dried fruit such as raisins, cranberries, or cherries, depending on what bugs you want."
8. **Homemade trail mix** — "Skip the pre-made trail mix and make your own. Use your favorite
   nuts and dried fruits, such as unsalted peanuts, cashews, walnuts, or sunflower seeds mixed
   with dried apples, pineapple, cherries, apricots, or raisins. Add whole-grain cereals to the
   mix, too."
9. **Potato person** — "Decorate half a baked potato. Use sliced cherry tomatoes, peas, and
   low-fat cheese on the potato to make a funny face."
10. **Put kids in charge** — "Ask your child to name new veggie or fruit creations. Let them
    arrange raw veggies or fruits into a fun shape or design."

## 2. Snack Ideas

**Source:** United States Department of Agriculture (USDA), Center for Nutrition Policy and
Promotion — *"10 tips Nutrition Education Series: snack tips for parents — 10 tips for healthy
snacking."* DG TipSheet No. 24, March 2013. Publicly published at ChooseMyPlate.gov. A WebSearch
result surfaced an unverified claim that this tipsheet carries an October 2016 revision; that claim
is not confirmed against the document itself (the extracted PDF prints only "March 2013") and is
not relied on here — see `04.2-RESEARCH.md`'s Assumptions Log for the re-verification flag.
Retrieved via a third-party mirror during this research pass, same caveat as §1: re-pull directly
from usda.gov/ChooseMyPlate.gov before Phase 5 sign-off. **Numbering below is Ritham's own; the
source's own layout is decorative, not a ranked order** (same note as §1).

Preamble, transcribed verbatim, **trimmed** per the §3 content rule below — the source's first two
sentences ("Snacks can help children get the nutrients needed to grow and maintain a healthy
weight. Prepare single-serving snacks for younger children to help them get just enough to satisfy
their hunger.") are weight-management and child-portion framing respectively, excluded under D-03;
only the source's third and fourth sentences are included: *"Let older kids make their own snacks
by keeping healthy foods in the kitchen. Visit ChooseMyPlate.gov to help you and your kids select
a satisfying snack."*

Nine of the source document's ten ideas, transcribed verbatim, three trimmed per §3's content rule
(marked below; see §3 for the tenth idea, excluded in full, and for the exact rule applied):

1. **Save time by slicing veggies** — "Store sliced vegetables in the refrigerator and serve with
   dips like hummus or low-fat dressing. Top half a whole-wheat English muffin with spaghetti
   sauce, chopped vegetables, and low-fat shredded mozzarella and melt in the microwave."
2. **Mix it up** — "For older school-age kids, mix dried fruit, unsalted nuts, and popcorn in a
   snack-size bag for a quick trail mix. Blend plain fat-free or low-fat yogurt with 100% fruit
   juice and frozen peaches for a tasty smoothie."
3. **Grab a glass of milk** — "A cup of low-fat or fat-free milk or milk alternative (soy milk)
   is an easy way to drink a healthy snack."
4. **Go for great whole grains** — "Offer whole-wheat breads, popcorn, and whole-oat cereals that
   are high in fiber and low in added sugars, saturated fat, and sodium. Limit refined-grain
   products such as snack bars, cakes, and sweetened cereals."
5. **Nibble on lean protein** — "Choose lean protein foods such as low-sodium deli meats,
   unsalted nuts, or eggs. Wrap sliced, low-sodium deli turkey or ham around an apple wedge.
   Store unsalted nuts in the pantry or peeled, hard-cooked (boiled) eggs in the refrigerator for
   kids to enjoy any time."
6. **Fruits are quick and easy** *(trimmed)* — "Fresh, frozen, dried, or canned fruits can be easy
   'grab-and-go' options that need little preparation. Offer whole fruit." *(Source continues:
   "...and limit the amount of 100% juice served." — omitted as a serve-amount directive under
   D-03.)*
7. **Consider convenience** *(trimmed)* — "A single-serving container of low-fat or fat-free
   yogurt or individually wrapped string cheese..." *(Source continues: "...can be just enough for
   an after-school snack." — omitted as portion-sufficiency framing under D-03.)*
8. **Swap out the sugar** *(trimmed)* — "Keep healthier foods handy so kids avoid cookies,
   pastries, or candies between meals. Add seltzer water to 100% fruit juice instead of offering
   soda." *(Source reads "...a ½ cup of 100% fruit juice..." — the measured-amount figure "a ½ cup
   of" is omitted under D-03; the rest of the sentence is unchanged and still makes sense without
   it.)*
9. **Prepare homemade goodies** — "For homemade sweets, add dried fruits like apricots or raisins
   and reduce the amount of sugar. Adjust recipes that include fats like butter or shortening by
   using unsweetened applesauce or prune puree for half the amount of fat." *(Not trimmed — see §3:
   this is a recipe-composition ratio for a batch of food being prepared, not a statement about how
   much of the finished food a child is served or should eat, so it falls outside the rule below.
   Matches `docs/dietary-pattern.md`'s own precedent of including recipe/ingredient-substitution
   guidance without treating it as portion-of-food-eaten guidance.)*

## 3. Content Rule Applied, and Excluded/Trimmed Source Content (D-03 compliance)

**The rule, stated once and applied uniformly across §1/§2 above (not just to the item below):**
any source sentence or clause that directs *how much of a food a child is served, should eat, or
should be limited to* is excluded or trimmed — whether or not it carries a specific number. This
covers phrases like "how much is enough," "just enough to satisfy hunger," "control serving
sizes," "limit the amount of X served," and specific measured serve amounts of a consumed food
(e.g., "a ½ cup of juice"). It also covers general weight-management framing (e.g., "maintain a
healthy weight") regardless of whether any quantity accompanies it, per D-03's "no weight-
management content of any kind." **It does not cover** recipe-level ingredient-ratio substitutions
in a food being prepared (a technique for adjusting a dish's composition — see §2 Tip 9) — that is
a different kind of statement from telling a child how much of the finished food to eat, and
`docs/dietary-pattern.md`'s own protein-swap table already establishes this distinction as
in-scope content for this codebase.

Applying this rule uniformly:

- **§2's preamble** — the source's first two sentences dropped in full (weight-management framing;
  child-portion-sufficiency framing).
- **§2 Tip 6, Tip 7, Tip 8** — trimmed (the serve-amount/portion-sufficiency clause removed from
  each; the rest of each tip's verbatim text is kept and still reads sensibly). See the inline
  `(trimmed)` markers and omission notes in §2 above for exactly what was cut from each.
- **The tenth idea in DG TipSheet No. 24** ("keep an eye on the size" — verbatim: *"Snacks
  shouldn't replace a meal, so look for ways to help your kids understand how much is enough.
  Store snack-size bags in the cupboard and use them to control serving sizes."*) is **excluded in
  full**, not trimmed — nearly the entire tip is portion-sufficiency framing of exactly the kind
  this rule excludes, leaving no substantive remainder worth keeping once that framing is removed.
- **§2 Tip 9** ("half the amount of fat" in a homemade recipe) is **kept in full** — a recipe-batch
  ingredient ratio, not child-serving-size guidance; see the rule's explicit carve-out above.

Documented here (rather than silently trimmed/dropped with no trace) so a future content-catalog
review knows exactly what rule was applied and why each specific cut was made — matching
`dietary-pattern.md`'s own precedent of flagging inference/exclusion decisions inline (its
⚑1/⚑2/⚑3 footnotes) rather than leaving them implicit. This is Ritham's own conservative reading of
D-03 (the source text names no numeric calorie/gram figure for most of what was trimmed here); a
Phase 5 clinical/legal reviewer may judge some of the trimmed clauses acceptable to restore — see
`04.2-RESEARCH.md`'s Assumptions Log (A2) and Open Questions (2).

## 4. Movement Ideas

**Source:** Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) / U.S. Department of Health and
Human Services (HHS) — *Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans*, 2nd edition (2018), as
presented on CDC's "Physical Activity Guidelines for School-Aged Children and Adolescents" and
"What Counts for Children and Teens" pages, ages 6-17. Confirmed against two independent CDC pages
presenting the same figures, cross-consistent.

**Daily target, transcribed verbatim:** *"Children and adolescents (age 6-17) need at least 60
minutes of physical activity every day."* / *"60 minutes or more of moderate-to-vigorous intensity
physical activity each day."*

**The three required components each week, transcribed verbatim:** *"Children and adolescents
need a mix of aerobic, muscle, and bone-strengthening activities each week."* /
*"Children and adolescents need moderate- or vigorous-intensity aerobic physical activity every
day. They also need muscle- and bone-strengthening activity at least 3 days each week."*

**Vigorous-intensity aerobic frequency, transcribed verbatim (this is the "3 days" figure that
applies to aerobic activity specifically — distinct from the muscle-/bone-strengthening "3 days"
figure quoted above, which is a separate weekly requirement):** *"Most of the 60 minutes or more
should be either moderate- or vigorous-intensity aerobic physical activity, and should include
vigorous-intensity physical activity on at least 3 days a week."* / *"At least 3 days a week
should include vigorous-intensity activities."*

**Intensity guidance, transcribed verbatim:** *"In general, at moderate intensity, children can
talk but not sing during the physical activity. At vigorous intensity, children can only say a
few words without pausing for a breath."*

### 4a. Aerobic activity ideas

Most of the daily 60 minutes or more should be moderate- or vigorous-intensity aerobic activity,
and should include vigorous-intensity aerobic activity on **at least 3 days a week** (not 3 minutes
a day — see the "Vigorous-intensity aerobic frequency" verbatim quote in §4's own preamble above).
Example activities, transcribed verbatim from CDC's own list:

- Moderate-intensity: "Brisk walking," "Bicycle riding (mostly on flat surfaces without many
  hills)," "Active recreation, such as hiking"
- Vigorous-intensity: "Running," "Jumping rope," "Sports such as soccer, basketball, swimming,
  and tennis"

### 4b. Muscle-strengthening activity ideas (at least 3 days a week)

Example activities, transcribed verbatim from CDC's own list: "Climbing on playground equipment,"
"Resistance exercises using body weight" (a companion CDC page also lists "doing push-ups" as an
example of this category).

### 4c. Bone-strengthening activity ideas (at least 3 days a week)

Example activities, transcribed verbatim from CDC's own list: "Hopping, skipping, jumping,"
"Jumping rope," "Running."

## 5. Suggested Swift Catalog Shape

For whoever plans/implements the `RithamCore` catalog this document feeds (mirrors
`NutritionGuidanceCatalog`'s `ReferenceFigure` citation shape — see that file's own header
comment for the discipline this shape enforces: no arithmetic, every string traceable to a named
publishing body):

```swift
public enum KidContentCategory: String, Sendable, Equatable, CaseIterable {
    case food
    case movement
}

/// A citable source attribution, one per idea -- mirrors `ReferenceFigure`'s
/// label/value/publishingBody shape but for a transcribed idea rather than a numeric figure.
public struct KidContentCitation: Sendable, Equatable {
    public let documentTitle: String       // e.g. "10 tips: veggies and fruits"
    public let documentIdentifier: String  // e.g. "USDA DG TipSheet No. 11"
    public let publishingBody: String      // e.g. "USDA Center for Nutrition Policy and Promotion"
    public let publicationDate: String     // e.g. "June 2011"
}

/// One browsable idea (KIDCONTENT-01). `body` is the verbatim transcribed tip text -- never
/// paraphrased, never containing a calorie/macro/portion/BMI figure (D-03).
public struct KidContentIdea: Sendable, Equatable, Identifiable {
    public let id: String          // stable slug, e.g. "food.smoothie-creations"
    public let category: KidContentCategory
    public let title: String
    public let body: String
    public let citation: KidContentCitation
}

public enum KidContentCatalog {
    public static let ideas: [KidContentIdea] = [ /* one entry per §1/§2/§4 item above */ ]

    public static func ideas(in category: KidContentCategory) -> [KidContentIdea] {
        ideas.filter { $0.category == category }
    }
}
```

Nineteen `food`-category ideas (ten from §1, nine from §2) and three `movement`-category idea
groups (§4a/4b/4c, each expressible as one idea per example activity or one idea per subcategory —
left to the planner/executor, no reference mockup constrains this) map directly onto this shape.
